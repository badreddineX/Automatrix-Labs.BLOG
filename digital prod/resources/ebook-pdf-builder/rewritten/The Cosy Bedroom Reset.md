# Chapter 1: Bedding

## Chapter Overview

Bedding is the starting point of the Cosy Bedroom Reset because it delivers the highest return for the least effort and risk. The bed is the largest object in a bedroom, and bedding covers the largest surface area of colour and texture in the room — so changing it changes more of what your eye registers than any other single purchase. This chapter sets out a three-layer bedding system (base, middle, top) and gives guidance on colour choice and pillow quantities.

Because bedding requires no drilling, no adhesive, and no landlord permission of any kind, it's the natural first step in any rental-safe room refresh — nothing here touches the walls or fixtures.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Choose a duvet cover colour that reads as warm and considered rather than clinical or "hotel-like."
- Build a three-layer bedding system (base, middle, top) with an approximate budget for each layer.
- Determine the right number of pillows for a double or king bed.

## Key Concepts

- **Highest-ROI change:** The bedding change delivers the greatest visual impact relative to cost and effort, because it covers the largest surface area of any single item in the room.
- **Three-layer system:** A structured way of building up bedding — base (sheets/pillowcases), middle (duvet cover + duvet), and top (throw blanket) — rather than buying pieces at random.
- **Warm neutral:** A colour category (cream, oatmeal, soft grey, muted earth tone) recommended for duvet covers because it avoids the two failure modes of white (shows stains, looks institutional) and black (fades, shows dust).

## Main Content

### Choosing the Duvet Cover

#### Important Details
- Choose a warm neutral: cream, oatmeal, soft grey, or a muted earth tone.
- Avoid white — it shows stains and can read as impersonal, like a hotel.
- Avoid black — it fades over time and shows dust readily.

### The Three-Layer System

#### Important Details
1. **Base:** Fitted sheet + flat sheet + pillowcases. Cotton or cotton-linen blend. £15-25 at Dunelm or M&S.
2. **Middle:** Duvet cover + duvet, in a warm colour. £25-40 at Dunelm or IKEA.
3. **Top:** Throw blanket at the foot of the bed, adding texture and warmth. £10-15 at Dunelm or Primark.

### Pillow Quantities

#### Important Details
- Double bed: 4-6 pillows total.
- King bed: 6-8 pillows total.
- Composition: two sleeping pillows per person, plus decorative pillows.

This chapter establishes bedding as the foundation of the Cosy Bedroom Reset, and the first task to tackle because it delivers the greatest visual transformation for the effort involved. # Chapter 2: Lighting

## Chapter Overview

This chapter addresses one of the most common problems in rental bedrooms: reliance on a single, harsh overhead ceiling light. It explains why that default lighting works against a restful atmosphere, and lays out a three-part lighting system — bedside lamp, floor lamp, and fairy lights — built around warm-toned bulbs.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Explain why a single ceiling light is poor lighting for a bedroom.
- Identify the three lighting elements needed to replace it, with approximate costs.
- Apply the "golden rule" of never using the ceiling light in a cosy bedroom setup.

## Key Concepts

- **Warm bulb (2700K):** A bulb colour temperature recommended as non-negotiable for bedside lamps, because it produces a warmer, more relaxing light than cooler-toned bulbs.
- **Ambient light:** Light that fills a room softly without harsh direct glare, provided here by a floor lamp.
- **The golden rule:** Never turn on the ceiling light; use lamps and fairy lights instead.

## Main Content

### Why the Ceiling Light Fails

#### Important Details
- A single ceiling light is described as the worst possible lighting for sleep.
- It is harsh and unflattering, and signals "wake up" to the brain rather than encouraging rest.

### The Three-Part Lighting System

#### Important Details
- **Bedside lamp:** Every bedroom needs at least one. Use a warm bulb (2700K) — described as non-negotiable. £10-20 at Primark, IKEA, or Dunelm.
- **Floor lamp:** Adds ambient light that fills the room without being harsh. £15-25 at IKEA or Argos.
- **Fairy lights:** Draped along the headboard or window frame. Use warm white, not cool white. £5-10 at Primark or The Range.

- Layer three light sources (bedside lamp, floor lamp, fairy lights) instead of one central overhead light.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter tackles bedroom lighting, starting from the observation that the standard single ceiling light found in most rentals is the worst possible choice for a space meant to promote rest. Overhead ceiling light is harsh and unflattering, and its brightness and angle send a "wake up" signal to the brain rather than encouraging the wind-down that a bedroom should support.

In place of the ceiling light, the chapter proposes a three-part lighting system, each piece chosen for a different function in the room. First is the bedside lamp, which every bedroom needs at minimum one of. The bulb temperature matters here specifically: a warm bulb rated at 2700K is described as non-negotiable, since cooler bulb temperatures undermine the relaxing effect the lamp is meant to provide. A bedside lamp costs roughly £10-20 and can be found at Primark, IKEA, or Dunelm.

Second is the floor lamp, which serves a different purpose from the bedside lamp: rather than providing a focused pool of light, it adds ambient light that fills the room evenly without the harshness of an overhead fixture. A floor lamp costs approximately £15-25 and is available at IKEA or Argos.

Third is fairy lights, draped along the headboard or the window frame for a soft, decorative glow. As with the bedside lamp, the colour temperature matters — warm white is specified, not cool white, to keep the overall lighting scheme consistent and relaxing. Fairy lights cost around £5-10 from Primark or The Range.

The chapter closes with what it calls the golden rule of bedroom lighting: never turn on the ceiling light. Instead, rely on the combination of bedside lamp, floor lamp, and fairy lights established in this chapter. Together, these three sources replace the single harsh overhead light with layered, warm, adjustable lighting — a foundational change that, like the bedding in Chapter 1, requires no drilling or permanent alteration to the room.

# Chapter 3: Curtains

## Chapter Overview

This chapter covers curtains, described as the second-largest surface area in the bedroom after the bed itself. It explains how curtains are hung for maximum visual effect, what fabric to choose for a bedroom specifically, and how to hang curtains without drilling into a rental property's walls or window frame.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Apply the correct hanging technique to make a ceiling look higher.
- Choose appropriate curtain fabric for a bedroom, including the role of blackout lining.
- Select a no-drill hanging method suited to the weight of the curtains.

## Key Concepts

- **Hanging rule:** Hanging the curtain rod as close to the ceiling as possible, with curtains touching the floor, to make the ceiling appear higher.
- **Blackout fabric:** Curtain fabric that blocks light and reduces noise, described as essential for bedrooms specifically.
- **No-drill solution:** A tension rod (for lightweight curtains) or adhesive brackets (for heavier ones), allowing curtains to be hung without damaging rental walls or frames.

## Main Content

#### Important Details
- Hang the rod as close to the ceiling as possible — this makes the ceiling look higher.

- **Cotton or linen:** Medium weight, machine washable. Best layered with blackout lining.

### No-Drill Hanging Methods

#### Important Details
- Tension rod inside the window frame, suited to lightweight curtains.
- Adhesive brackets, suited to heavier curtains.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter turns to curtains, the second-largest surface area in the bedroom after the bed, and explains that the difference between curtains that make a room feel cheap and curtains that make it feel finished comes down to hanging technique and fabric choice, not just colour or pattern.

The hanging rule given is straightforward: mount the rod as close to the ceiling as possible, which creates the visual illusion of a taller ceiling, and let the curtains hang all the way down to touch the floor. This combination — high rod, floor-length drop — is presented as the single biggest factor in whether curtains read as polished or cheap.

On fabric, the chapter distinguishes between two categories. Blackout fabric is called essential specifically for bedrooms, since it blocks external light, reduces noise from outside, and directly helps sleep quality — a function that matters more in a bedroom than in any other room of the home. Blackout curtains cost roughly £20-30 from Dunelm, Argos, or IKEA. The second category is cotton or linen fabric, a medium-weight, machine-washable option that the chapter recommends layering with blackout lining behind it, combining the aesthetic softness of cotton or linen with the functional benefit of blackout.

Finally, the chapter addresses the practical rental problem of hanging curtains without drilling into the window frame or wall. Two no-drill solutions are offered, chosen based on curtain weight: a tension rod fitted inside the window frame for lightweight curtains, and adhesive brackets for heavier curtain sets that a tension rod might not support. Both methods allow a full curtain upgrade — fabric, blackout lining, and correct hanging height — without any permanent alteration to the property, keeping the change fully reversible and deposit-safe, consistent with the bedding and lighting chapters before it.
