## Introduction

Moving is one of the most stressful life events. Psychologists rank it alongside divorce and job loss on the stress scale. Not because moving is inherently difficult — but because it combines physical exhaustion, financial pressure, and emotional upheaval all at once.

You're leaving a place that knew you. A kitchen where you knew which drawer had the utensils. A bathroom where you knew how the shower temperature worked. A neighbourhood where you knew the closest coffee shop. All of that is gone, and none of the new information has arrived yet.

This guide exists because the first week in a new apartment sets the tone for your entire tenancy. The decisions you make in the first seven days — unpacking order, address registration, utility setup, landlord relationship — determine whether you spend the next 12 months feeling settled or scrambling.

Over the next pages, you'll learn exactly what to do each day of your first week. Not everything — just the essentials. By day seven, you'll have a functioning home, a clear head, and the confidence that you've got this.

# Chapter 1: The First Hour

## Chapter Overview

This chapter covers the single most consequential hour of the entire move: the one before the moving truck arrives, and the moments right after you receive your keys. It explains why documenting the apartment's pre-existing condition, confirming your parking spot, and testing utilities before unloading a single box protects both your money and your first-night comfort.

The chapter's core argument is that most movers waste this hour by carrying boxes in random order, while the smartest movers use it to set up the systems — evidence, documentation, working utilities — that make the rest of the week easier. It closes with a real example of a deposit dispute that could have been avoided with 15 minutes of photography.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Document an apartment's pre-existing condition in a way that holds up in a tenancy dispute.
- Identify which utilities, fixtures, and building details to confirm before moving boxes in.
- Assemble the minimum purchase and document list needed before the truck arrives.
- Recognize the two most common first-hour mistakes and avoid them.

## Key Concepts

- **Critical moment:** A point where a small investment of effort creates an outsized return — in this case, 15 minutes of photography that can protect hundreds or thousands of dollars in deposit money.
- **Move-in inspection report:** A formal, landlord-provided document recording the unit's condition at move-in, which must be filled out, photographed, and submitted within your province's deadline.
- **Timestamped evidence:** A record (such as an emailed photo set) that establishes when damage documentation was created, making it admissible in a tenancy dispute.

## Main Content

### Before the Truck Arrives

#### Important Details

1. **Walk through with your phone camera.** Photograph every pre-existing scuff, dent, crack, and stain. Open every cabinet. Flush every toilet. Turn on every tap. Check for leaks under every sink. Email the photos to yourself with the date. If you don't do this, your landlord may claim your damage deposit for marks that existed before you moved in.
2. **Mark your parking spot.** Many new renters get towed on move-in day because they assumed their parking was where they parked during the viewing. Confirm the spot number or location with your landlord or building manager.
3. **Test the utilities.** Turn on lights in every room. Check that outlets work. Flush toilets. Run the shower. If something doesn't work, call the landlord before you move a single box in.

### What to Buy Before the Truck Arrives

#### Important Details

- A plunger ($8-10)
- Toilet paper and paper towels
- One towel and one set of sheets for the first night
- Basic cleaning supplies (all-purpose cleaner, sponge, dish soap, garbage bags)
- A box cutter or scissors

### Documents to Collect

#### Important Details

- Your lease — scanned and stored on your phone and in cloud storage
- The move-in inspection report — filled out, photographed, and submitted within your province's deadline
- Your landlord's emergency contact number
- Your tenant insurance policy number and claim line

### Design Insight

The first hour is what designers call a **"critical moment"** — a point where a small investment of effort creates an outsized return. Photographing the apartment takes 15 minutes. It can save you hundreds or thousands of dollars in deposit disputes. The return on that 15 minutes is higher than almost anything else you'll do during the entire move.

## Examples

**Situation:** Mike moved into a Toronto one-bedroom (500 sq ft) that looked clean during the viewing.

**Explanation:** After moving in, he noticed a crack in the bathroom tile and a stain on the living room carpet, but had not photographed either before moving boxes in. He emailed his landlord immediately with photos taken *after* the move — not before.

**Result:** The landlord claimed the damage was pre-existing but could not prove it either way. Mike lost $200 of his deposit. He now photographs every apartment before moving a single box in, saving the photos in a folder called "Move-In Evidence" with the date.

### Tips

- Take 10 photos of your empty apartment right now and email them to yourself.
- Flush every toilet and run every tap — takes 5 minutes.
- Buy a plunger before you need one.
- Email photos to yourself with the subject line "Move-In Photos — [Address] — [Date]" to create a timestamped, dispute-admissible record.
- Fill out the move-in inspection report within your province's deadline (usually 7-14 days), photograph the completed report, and submit it in writing.

### Important

- **Not photographing the apartment** is the single most common cause of deposit loss. Photograph everything before you move in.
- **Assuming the parking spot is obvious** can get you towed on move-in day, adding $200+ to your moving costs. Confirm the spot number with the landlord.

## Best Practices

- Document before you unload — evidence gathered after boxes are in no longer proves pre-existing condition.
- Use email (not just phone storage) to create a timestamped, backed-up record.
- Complete and submit the official move-in inspection report within your province's deadline, not just informal photos.
- Buy the minimum first-night kit in advance so you're not making a store run after a full day of moving.

## Key Takeaways

- The first hour sets the tone for the entire move.
- Photograph everything, test everything, and buy the essentials before the truck arrives.
- Fifteen minutes of preparation saves hours of frustration — and can save hundreds of dollars in deposit disputes.

Testing utilities — lights, outlets, toilets, showers — before moving boxes in is the third priority, because problems are far easier to report and fix before the apartment is filled with belongings.

# Chapter 2: Unpack by Zone

## Chapter Overview

This chapter reframes the unpacking process around zones of immediate need rather than box order or room-by-room convenience. It argues that unpacking the kitchen first — the intuitive choice — leaves the bedroom inaccessible for days, forcing renters to sleep on the couch and feel like they're camping in their own home. Instead, it proposes a four-zone sequence — bedroom, bathroom, kitchen, everything else — built around what a person needs to function, not what's easiest to organize.

The chapter also introduces the "one-week rule" as a decluttering mechanism: if a box hasn't been opened within a week, its contents are probably not needed, and most people can donate or store 30-40% of what they moved without ever missing it.

## Learning Objectives

- Apply the four-zone unpacking sequence (bedroom, bathroom, kitchen, everything else) to a real move.
- Explain why unpacking by box order is inefficient compared to unpacking by zone.
- Use the "one-week rule" to identify boxes that can be deferred or donated.
- Build a "first-night box" that eliminates late-night searching through unopened boxes.

## Key Concepts

- **Layering:** An interior design principle where a room is furnished with essentials first, then decor and accessories are added over time — applied here to unpacking in stages rather than all at once.
- **One-week rule:** If a box hasn't been unpacked after one week, its contents are probably not immediately needed and can be stored, donated, or deferred.
- **First-night box:** A single labeled box packed with everything needed for the first 24 hours, so nothing essential is buried in an unopened box on move-in night.

## Main Content

### The Four Unpacking Zones

#### Important Details

- **Zone 1 — Bedroom (unpack first).** Set up the bed frame and mattress, unpack sheets and pillows, hang one lamp. This should be the first thing done after the boxes are in.
- **Zone 2 — Bathroom.** Unpack towels, toiletries, and the shower curtain. Use a temporary caddy rather than organizing the vanity perfectly — organization can wait.
- **Zone 3 — Kitchen.** Unpack only first-week essentials: one set of dishes, two pots, basic utensils, a coffee maker. Everything else stays boxed until you know where you want it.
- **Zone 4 — Everything else.** With essentials settled, deal with remaining boxes at your own pace over the next month.

### The One-Week Rule

#### Important Details

If a box hasn't been unpacked after one week, its contents probably aren't needed. Stack untouched boxes in a corner. Most people find they can donate or store 30-40% of what they brought without ever missing it.

### Design Insight

Interior designers use a principle called **"layering"** — you don't furnish a room all at once. You start with the essentials (bed, sofa, table) and add layers over time (art, accessories, decor). The same principle applies to unpacking: start with the essentials, add layers as you settle in.

## Tables

| Zone | Priority | What to Unpack | What to Defer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Bedroom | First | Bed frame, mattress, sheets, pillows, one lamp | Decor, extra linens |
| 2 — Bathroom | Second | Towels, toiletries, shower curtain | Vanity organization |
| 3 — Kitchen | Third | One dish set, two pots, basic utensils, coffee maker | Everything else |
| 4 — Everything else | Ongoing | At your own pace | N/A |

## Lists

**First-night box contents ("OPEN FIRST"):**
- Sheets
- Pillow
- Towel
- Toiletries
- Phone charger
- Change of clothes
- Coffee mug

## Examples

**Situation:** Sarah unpacked her Vancouver studio (400 sq ft) by box order on move-in day, opening kitchen boxes first because they were on top.

**Explanation:** By evening her kitchen was organized, but she had no bed set up, so she slept on the floor.

**Result:** On her next move, she unpacked by zone — bedroom first, bathroom second, kitchen third — and was asleep in her own bed by 9 PM on move-in day.

### Tips

- Set up your bed first, before anything else.
- Unpack one box from each zone rather than all boxes from one zone.
- Stack untouched boxes in a corner after one week.
- Pack a "first-night box" labeled "OPEN FIRST" with everything needed for the first 24 hours.
- Use a cardboard box as a temporary drawer until you find the right furniture.
- Don't buy any furniture or storage for the first month — live in the space first, then buy what you actually need.

### Important

- **Unpacking by box order** is a common mistake — the box on top is rarely the box you need. Unpack by zone instead.
- **Trying to organize everything perfectly on day one** is premature. You don't know your new space yet; live in it for a month before making permanent decisions.

## Best Practices

- Always unpack the bedroom first, regardless of how "logical" starting with the kitchen feels.
- Use temporary solutions (a cardboard-box drawer, a bathroom caddy) rather than perfecting organization on day one.
- Apply the one-week rule ruthlessly to avoid an apartment cluttered with unnecessary boxes.

## Key Takeaways

- Unpack by zone, not by box order.
- Bedroom first, bathroom second, kitchen third — everything else can wait.
- A first-night box prevents digging through 20 boxes at 11 PM.
- If a box goes untouched for a week, you probably don't need what's inside.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter argues that the order in which you unpack determines whether your first days in a new apartment feel functional or chaotic. The default approach — opening whatever box is on top, often starting with the kitchen — leaves the bedroom inaccessible for days, which the chapter identifies as a major source of unnecessary discomfort. Instead, it proposes unpacking by zone: bedroom first (bed frame, mattress, sheets, pillows, one lamp), bathroom second (towels, toiletries, shower curtain, using a temporary caddy rather than a fully organized vanity), kitchen third (only first-week essentials — one dish set, two pots, basic utensils, a coffee maker), and everything else last, handled at a comfortable pace over the following weeks.

The reasoning behind this order is functional, not aesthetic: a person needs to sleep, shower, and eat before anything else matters. The chapter draws a parallel to the interior design principle of "layering," where a room is furnished with essentials first and decor is added in stages over time — the same logic applied to unpacking rather than furnishing.

A second major concept in this chapter is the "one-week rule": if a box remains unopened after a week in the new apartment, its contents likely aren't needed in the near term. The chapter notes that most people find they can donate or store 30-40% of what they moved without ever missing it, which reframes unpacking not just as a task but as an opportunity to declutter.

The chapter also introduces the "first-night box" — a single box, clearly labeled "OPEN FIRST," packed with sheets, a pillow, a towel, toiletries, a phone charger, a change of clothes, and a coffee mug. This avoids the common frustration of digging through twenty identical boxes at 11 PM looking for a toothbrush.

Sarah's real example from a 400-square-foot Vancouver studio illustrates the cost of getting the order wrong: by unpacking the kitchen first because it was physically on top of the pile, she ended up sleeping on the floor on her first night. On a subsequent move, following the zone order let her be asleep in her own bed by 9 PM.

The chapter closes with two common mistakes to avoid — unpacking by box order instead of by zone, and trying to organize everything perfectly on day one before you actually understand how you'll use the space — and reiterates that the smartest approach is to handle essentials first and let everything else unfold gradually over the following month.

# Chapter 3: Address Registration

## Chapter Overview

This chapter treats address updates as a single, time-boxed task rather than an ongoing chore: "Day 2 is address day." It walks through the six categories of accounts and institutions that need a new address — Canada Post, provincial health cards, driver's licence and vehicle registration, the CRA, insurance, and banks/credit cards/subscriptions — and explains the financial consequences of missing each one, from lost mail to missed government benefit payments.

The central tool the chapter recommends is a single running list ("Address Update List") kept on your phone, framed as a reusable system rather than a one-time task.

## Learning Objectives

- List the six categories of accounts that require an address update after a move.
- Explain why Canada Post mail forwarding is not a substitute for updating individual accounts.
- Identify the financial risk of failing to update your address with the CRA.
- Build a reusable "Address Update List" system for this move and future moves.

## Key Concepts

- **Single source of truth:** One master list containing every account that needs an address update, so nothing has to be remembered from scratch.
- **Mail forwarding window:** Canada Post forwarding lasts only 3-6 months; after that, mail sent to the old address is no longer redirected.
- **CRA benefit payments:** Government payments (Canada Child Benefit, GST/HST credit, Climate Action Incentive) are mailed and tied to the address on file with the CRA.

## Main Content

### Day 2 Address Tasks

#### Important Details

1. **Canada Post** — redirect your mail ($50 for 3 months, $75 for 6 months). Catches anything you miss; done online in 10 minutes.
2. **Provincial health card** — update online or in person. Process varies by province (Ontario: ServiceOntario; BC: ICBC; Alberta: Alberta Health).
3. **Driver's licence and vehicle registration** — each province has a deadline (usually 30-90 days). Fines for late registration vary.
4. **CRA** — update online through My Account. Critical for tax season and benefit payments like the Canada Child Benefit, GST/HST credit, and Climate Action Incentive.
5. **Insurance** — update tenant insurance with the new address; also update car insurance, as rates may change based on the new neighbourhood.
6. **Bank, credit cards, subscriptions** — update billing addresses, starting with accounts that send physical bills.

### The Address Update List

#### Important Details

Create a note on your phone called "Address Update List" with every account that needs your address. Check off each one as you update it. This list saves hours on every future move.

### Design Insight

The best system for address changes is what designers call **"a single source of truth"** — one list that contains everything. Instead of remembering which accounts need updating, you have one list that tells you. Create it once, use it for every move.

## Tables

| Category | Action | Typical Deadline | Consequence of Missing It |
|---|---|---|---|
| Canada Post | Redirect mail | Set up before/at move | Mail lost or delayed after forwarding window |
| Provincial health card | Update online/in person | Varies by province | Delayed correspondence, ID mismatch |
| Driver's licence / vehicle registration | Update address | 30-90 days | Fines |
| CRA | Update via My Account | Immediate | Missed benefit payments (e.g., GST/HST credit) |
| Insurance (tenant + car) | Update address | Immediate | Incorrect rate, potential coverage issues |
| Bank / credit cards / subscriptions | Update billing address | Immediate | Missed bills, service disruption |

## Lists

**Address Update List should include:**
- Canada Post mail forwarding
- Provincial health card
- Driver's licence
- Vehicle registration
- CRA (My Account)
- Tenant insurance
- Car insurance
- Bank accounts
- Credit cards
- Subscriptions

## Examples

**Situation:** James, in a Montreal one-bedroom (500 sq ft), forgot to update his address with the CRA.

**Explanation:** He missed a GST credit cheque that was sent to his old address; by the time he realized, the cheque had already been cashed by someone else.

**Result:** He created an "Address Update List" with 15 accounts and, on his next move, checked off each one on day 2. He has since used the same list for three moves, adding new accounts as he opens them.

### Tips

- Create the Address Update List right now — open your phone and type "Address Update List."
- Update Canada Post first, since it catches anything else you miss.
- Set a reminder for your province's driver's licence deadline.
- Keep the list in a note app (Apple Notes, Google Keep) and add new accounts as you open them, so it grows more complete with each move.
- One month after moving, check whether any mail is still going to your old address — if so, you missed an account.
- Switch as many accounts as possible to paperless billing to reduce the number of things you need to update in future moves.

### Important

- **Assuming mail forwarding is enough** is a mistake — Canada Post forwarding lasts only 3-6 months, after which mail goes to your old address. Update each account individually.
- **Forgetting the CRA** is a costly mistake since it sends benefit payments by mail. Update it first among your financial accounts.

## Best Practices

- Treat "Day 2" as a dedicated address-update day rather than spreading the task out.
- Maintain one running list rather than relying on memory.
- Prioritize Canada Post and the CRA first, since they have the broadest and costliest consequences if missed.
- Reuse and expand the same list on every future move.

## Key Takeaways

- Day 2 is address day — one list, one day, done.
- Canada Post forwarding is a safety net, not a substitute for updating individual accounts.
- The CRA should be updated promptly to avoid missing benefit payments.
- A single, reusable "Address Update List" saves hours on this move and every future move.

# Chapter 4: Utilities

## Chapter Overview

This chapter addresses the two utilities that Canadian renters typically must set up themselves, separate from the landlord: electricity/hydro and internet. It stresses timing as the core lesson — hydro should be arranged before move-in day, while internet installation should be scheduled for Day 3 or 4 rather than Day 1, since the first two days are too chaotic for a technician visit.

It also covers provincial variation in hydro providers and practical considerations like choosing fibre over cable and checking for building-exclusive internet deals.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify which utilities are typically the renter's responsibility to set up in a Canadian apartment.
- Explain why internet installation should be scheduled for Day 3 or 4 rather than move-in day.
- Recognize your province's hydro/electricity provider.
- Build a utility setup timeline starting one week before move-in.

## Key Concepts

- **Utility timeline:** A staged schedule — hydro set up a week before move-in, internet scheduled 3-5 days before, both confirmed and tested on move-in day.
- **Building-exclusive internet deal:** An arrangement some newer buildings have with a single internet provider, worth checking before shopping around independently.
- **Fibre vs. cable:** Fibre optic internet is generally faster and more reliable than cable, often at a comparable price.

## Main Content

### Setting Up Hydro/Electricity

#### Important Details

Set up at least a few days before move-in. Provincial providers include:
- Ontario: Hydro One, Toronto Hydro, or local utility
- British Columbia: BC Hydro
- Alberta: Enmax, Epcor, or Direct Energy
- Quebec: Hydro-Québec
- Nova Scotia: Nova Scotia Power

### Scheduling Internet

#### Important Details

Schedule installation for Day 3 or 4, not Day 1 — the first two days are too chaotic for a technician visit. Check if your building has an exclusive deal with one internet provider (many new buildings do). If fibre optic is available, choose it over cable.

### Utility Timeline

#### Important Details

- 1 week before: Set up hydro
- 3-5 days before: Schedule internet
- Move-in day: Confirm hydro is active; test all outlets and lights
- Day 3-4: Internet installation

### Design Insight

The best time to set up utilities is **before you need them.** Setting up hydro a week before move-in costs nothing and ensures you arrive to a lit apartment. Waiting until move-in day means you might spend your first night in the dark.

## Examples

**Situation:** Emma, moving into a Calgary one-bedroom (500 sq ft), assumed her landlord handled hydro.

**Explanation:** She arrived on move-in day to a dark apartment, with power not set to be connected for three days.

**Result:** She called the utility company and paid an expedited connection fee ($50) to get power on the next day, rather than waiting three nights in the dark. She now confirms what's included in the lease and sets up everything else before move-in day.

### Tips

- Call the utility company one week before move-in — takes 15 minutes.
- Schedule internet for Day 3 or 4, not move-in day.
- Test all outlets on move-in day.
- Choose fibre optic over cable when available — faster, more reliable, often the same price.
- Check with your building manager for an exclusive internet provider deal before shopping around.
- Set up automatic payments for hydro and internet on day one.

### Important

- **Assuming utilities are included** is a mistake — check your lease. If hydro isn't listed as included, you need to set it up yourself.

## Chapter Overview

This chapter gives renters a practical framework for deciding which repairs to handle themselves and which to escalate to the landlord.
