## The Problem This Kit Solves

Most colour advice online was written for American homes with south-facing floor-to-ceiling windows, or Scandinavian interiors where brilliant white works precisely because the architecture is designed around it. Neither translates to a standard British terrace with a 1970s bay window, a north-facing aspect, and carpet that's technically beige.

Dulux's 2024 Colour of the Year research found that **71% of UK homeowners say they find it difficult to choose a living room colour** because the colours they love in shops or online look completely different at home.

That isn't indecision. That's the British light problem — and it's what this kit is built around.

**A note on paint names:** every scheme lists the colour family first (what to look for) and specific paint names second, across a premium and a budget brand. Ranges get discontinued and reformulated — treat the named colours as a starting reference, not a shopping list to follow blindly.

## PAGE 1 — Why UK Light Changes Everything

British light is cool, grey, and low-angled for most of the year. Three consequences follow, and every scheme in this kit is built on them:

☐ **Warm undertones beat cool ones.** Cool greys go flat and cold after 3pm in a north-facing room. Colours carrying yellow, brown, or green warmth stay warm all day

☐ **Off-white trim beats brilliant white.** Stark brilliant white next to any wall colour reads as a hard edge under grey light. Off-white softens it and almost always looks more considered

☐ **Artificial light does half the work.** Most of the British year is spent looking at these colours under a bulb, not daylight. A scheme that works at 2700K can look institutional at 4000K — sort the lighting before committing to the paint

**The aspect test — do this before choosing anything:**

| Your Room Faces | What It Does to Colour | What to Choose |
|---|---|---|
| **North** | Cools everything; greys go blue, whites go clinical | Strong warm undertones — greige, sage, ochre, terracotta |
| **South** | Warms and brightens; most colours flatter | Almost anything; cooler tones finally work here |
| **East** | Bright cool morning, flat afternoon | Warm neutrals that hold up in the afternoon |
| **West** | Flat morning, warm golden evening | Colours you'll mostly see in the evening — deep greens, ochre, charcoal |

## PAGE 2 — The Sampling Method (Do Not Skip This)

The single most expensive mistake in decorating is buying five litres of paint from a 3 cm swatch.

☐ **Paint samples onto A4 card, not the wall.** Two coats. Card lets you move the colour around the room; a patch painted directly on the wall is trapped where you put it and shows through the final coat

☐ **Test at least three candidates.** They look identical in the tin and entirely different at 7pm

☐ **Move each card to every wall.** The same colour on a wall facing the window and a wall facing away is effectively two different colours

☐ **Look at them at four times: morning, midday, late afternoon, and evening under your actual bulbs.** The evening check is the one people skip and the one that decides whether you'll like it

☐ **Hold the card against your largest fixed element** — the sofa, the carpet, the worktop. The scheme has to work with what you're not replacing

☐ **Live with the cards for a full week** before buying a tin

☐ **Check the trim colour at the same time.** Trim is roughly a fifth of what you see in a room and it's usually decided by default

**Sample pot cost:** roughly £2–7 depending on brand. Testing four colours properly costs less than £30 and prevents a £150 mistake plus a weekend of repainting.

## PAGE 4 — The Five Safest Schemes, In Detail

### 1. Warm Greige + Off-White Trim + Natural Oak
**Walls:** warm greige — e.g. Dulux Goose Down, Farrow & Ball Elephant's Breath
**Trim:** off-white — e.g. Farrow & Ball Wimborne White, Dulux Timeless
**Wood:** natural oak or light ash
**Textiles:** cream, sand, warm white

**Why it works:** greige sits between grey and beige, pulling yellow and brown warmth into a colour that still reads neutral. Cool greys go flat after 3pm in a north-facing room; greige stays warm all day. It's the most reliable starting point in British homes for a practical reason, not a fashionable one.

**Accents:** dusty terracotta cushions, a jute rug, and one dark piece — a charcoal floor lamp — to stop it reading too soft.

---

### 4. Sage Green + Warm White + Rattan
**Walls:** sage — e.g. Farrow & Ball Mizzle, Little Greene Sage, Crown Garden Party
**Trim:** warm white, not bright white
**Materials:** rattan and cane accents
**Textiles:** cream, oat, natural linen

**Why it works:** sage carries the freshness of green without the visual weight of darker tones, and its yellow undertone warms up under artificial light rather than going flat. It's the most seasonally flexible colour in this kit — fresh in summer with the curtains open, cosy in winter under warm bulbs.

**Accents:** dusty pink, terracotta, or mustard. Avoid cool greys and blues, which pull the whole scheme cold.

---

### 5. Warm White + Natural Wood + Texture Layers
**Walls and trim:** warm white — e.g. Farrow & Ball Wimborne White, Dulux Natural Hessian, Little Greene Linen White
**Wood:** oak, pine, or ash

**Why it works:** all-white rooms are difficult in the UK — cool whites look clinical under grey skies. Warm whites, the ones carrying yellow, pink, or cream rather than blue, are among the most liveable schemes available.

**The condition:** this scheme lives or dies on texture. Without it the room looks underdressed. Chunky knit throws, a wool rug, linen curtains, and a woven basket or two are not optional here — they're the scheme.

**Trim tip:** matching wall and trim in the same warm white removes visual fragmentation and works especially well in smaller rooms.

---

### 2. Deep Forest Green + Brass + Cream Upholstery
**Walls:** muted forest green — e.g. Crown Eternity, Farrow & Ball Calke Green
**Upholstery:** cream or oatmeal
**Metals:** brass or antique gold

**Why it works:** this is the scheme that looks most expensive for the least money. The version that works in British homes is *not* the saturated bottle green on US interior blogs — it's a softer forest green with brown undertones. Pure cool greens go clinical under UK artificial light. Look for "sage" or "forest" in the name rather than "emerald" or "bottle."

**The renter-friendly version:** one wall — the chimney breast or the wall behind the sofa — reads more considered than four green walls in a standard British living room, and costs about £15 of paint.

---

### 11. Blue-Grey + Warm Cream + Woven Textiles
**Walls:** warm-undertoned blue-grey — e.g. Farrow & Ball Pavilion Gray, Little Greene Juniper Ash. Budget equivalents: Dulux Steel Symphony, Crown Pale Slate
**Trim and upholstery:** warm cream

**Why it works:** it's the scheme for wanting something more considered than greige without committing to a deep colour. Calm, atmospheric, and warmer than it sounds — *provided* the undertone is warm. A blue-grey with a cool undertone reads clinical, and that's the whole risk with this one.

**The condition:** woven textiles in cream, sand, and oatmeal supply the warmth the walls withhold. Skip them and the room goes cold.

## PAGE 5 — The Six Bolder Schemes

| Scheme | Walls (premium / budget) | The Rule That Makes It Work |
|---|---|---|
| **Navy + terracotta + linen** | Little Greene Hague Blue / Dulux Maritime Blue | Pair navy with *warm* accents — terracotta, rust, burnt orange. Navy with cool accents reads cold and institutional |
| **Terracotta + cream + dark wood** | F&B Dead Salmon / Dulux Copper Blush | Cream, never white. Dark wood, never light. The muted, slightly grey terracottas work; the saturated ones need more space than most rooms have |
| **Charcoal + blush + brass** | Little Greene Urbane Grey / Crown Storm | Keep blush to 20–30% of the scheme. This one depends almost entirely on lighting — cold overhead light kills it, warm 2700K lamps make it |
| **Ochre + white + teak** | F&B Sudbury Yellow / Dulux Gold Digger | Ochre is not yellow. Sort your lighting first — under 2700K it glows, under cool bulbs it goes institutional |
| **Dusky pink + warm grey + linen** | F&B Sulking Room Pink / Dulux Antique Pink | Everything in the room must be warm. One cool grey or bright white and it collapses. The muted, weathered pinks only |
| **Black + natural wood + white linen** | F&B Railings / Dulux Black | Light upholstery is mandatory, not optional. Multiple low lamps, never one overhead — that's the difference between dramatic and oppressive |

**Chimney-breast rule for all six:** every one of these works as a single feature wall before it works as a whole room. About £15–20 of paint, two weeks of living with it, then decide. This is also the only version of these schemes most renters should attempt.

## PAGE 6 — Printable Palette Cards

*Print, cut, and carry. One card per scheme — 11 cards, two per A4 sheet.*

**Each card contains:**
- The scheme name and its four colour blocks, printed at true proportion (wall, trim, upholstery, accent)
- Named paint references — one premium, one budget
- Best aspect (north / south / east / west)
- Risk level and approximate entry cost
- Two accent colours that work, and one to avoid

**How to use them:** take the cards to the paint shop and to the furniture shop. Hold the card against the sofa fabric, the carpet, the worktop sample. The card is a physical shortlist that stops you starting from scratch in every shop.

**Print note:** these cards carry printed colour blocks, so they are a *reference to the named paint*, not a colour match. Home printers do not reproduce paint colour accurately, and no printable can. Always confirm against a real sample pot or the manufacturer's own swatch before buying. *(This is stated on the cards themselves, not just here.)*
