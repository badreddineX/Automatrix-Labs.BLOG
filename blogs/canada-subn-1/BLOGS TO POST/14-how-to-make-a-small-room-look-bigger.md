Making a small room look bigger is not about fooling yourself — it is about using real design principles that change how the brain perceives space. Light, sightlines, scale, and contrast all affect how large a room feels. Understanding which levers to pull is what separates a cramped apartment from one that feels surprisingly open.

I have lived in 510 sq ft in Toronto for three years. In that time I have learned which changes actually move the needle and which are just magazine advice that does not translate to real apartments.

## 1. Keep Walls Light — Even If You Cannot Paint

Light walls are the single most effective way to make a small room feel larger. They reflect light, push visual boundaries outward, and make ceilings feel higher.

Most Canadian rental apartments already have white or off-white walls. If yours are a darker colour:
- Use a peel-and-stick removable wallpaper in a light colour or subtle pattern on the primary wall (~$40–$80 CAD per roll at Amazon.ca)
- Use large light-coloured art or a gallery wall to visually lighten a dark wall
- Add mirrors to reflect light across the space

If your walls are already light, keep them that way. Dark accent walls are a beautiful choice in large rooms. In a small room they make the space feel significantly smaller.

## 2. Use a Large Mirror in Every Room

A mirror reflects whatever is across from it, effectively doubling the visual depth of the room in that direction. Place it opposite a window and it also doubles the natural light.

**The rules:**
- Bigger is better. A small decorative mirror does less work than a large one
- Lean it against a wall rather than mounting it centred on a small wall section
- Place it where it will reflect the most attractive view (a window, a plant, or a well-styled corner — not a cluttered wall)

**Options:** IKEA HOVET full-length mirror (~$279 CAD) is the classic choice. Round wall mirrors from Amazon.ca from ~$45 CAD.

## 3. Choose Furniture With Visible Legs

Furniture that sits directly on the floor looks heavy and makes the room feel smaller. Furniture with visible legs lets you see the floor underneath, which makes the room feel lighter and larger.

This applies to every major piece:
- Sofa with legs instead of a platform sofa
- Bed frame with legs instead of a solid-base platform
- Console table on slim metal legs instead of a solid wood cabinet

The visual principle is simple: more floor visible = room feels larger.

## 4. Get the Rug Size Right

The single most common small room mistake is a rug that is too small. A small rug makes the room look smaller, not larger — it creates a visual island of furniture that looks like it's floating on bare floor.

**The rule:** in a living room, the front legs of the sofa and chairs must sit on the rug. In a bedroom, the rug should extend 50–60 cm on each side and at the foot of the bed.

A 160 × 230 cm rug is usually the minimum for a small living room. Light neutral colours work best — cream, natural jute, pale grey.

IKEA flat-weave rugs from ~$59 CAD. Wayfair Canada has a wider range.

## 5. Hang Curtains From Ceiling to Floor

This creates the illusion of ceiling height. The eye follows vertical lines upward — curtains that run from ceiling to floor make the ceiling feel taller than it actually is.

The mistake most people make: hanging the curtain rod just above the window frame and letting curtains end at the window sill or just below it. This anchors the ceiling at the window height, making the room feel lower.

**How to do it right:**
- Mount the rod 5–10 cm below the ceiling (or directly to the ceiling)
- Curtains fall to the floor with 1–2 cm clearance
- Use light, sheer fabric to keep natural light

IKEA curtain rods from ~$15 CAD. LILL sheer panels from ~$10 CAD per pair.

## 6. Reduce Furniture — Every Piece Counts

In a small room, every unnecessary piece of furniture reduces the space and increases the crowded feeling. The question to ask about every piece: if this weren't here, would the room function worse, or would it just have less stuff in it?

Common pieces to reconsider:
- An accent chair that no one sits in
- A coffee table that is too large for the room
- Side tables that are just surfaces for clutter
- A TV unit when a wall mount or low-profile stand would work
- A desk in the bedroom when the living area could hold one instead

Remove one piece and see how the room feels. It is almost always better.

## 7. Keep Sightlines Clear

A sightline is the unobstructed view from one point in the room to another. In a small room, clear sightlines from the entry to the far wall create the impression of depth.

Block those sightlines with tall furniture or cluttered surfaces and the room immediately feels smaller and more confined.

**Practical rules:**
- Nothing tall (over 120 cm) should sit in the path between the door and the far wall
- The area around the entry should be clear
- Glass furniture (coffee table, side table) maintains sightlines better than solid pieces

## 8. Use Vertical Lines to Add Height

Anything that creates vertical lines draws the eye upward and makes the ceiling feel taller. In a small room, going vertical is the easiest way to change the perceived height.

**Ways to add vertical lines:**
- Floor-to-ceiling curtains
- Tall bookshelves that reach close to the ceiling
- Vertical stacks of floating shelves rather than horizontal arrangements
- Vertically striped wallpaper or wallpaper with a strong vertical pattern

## 9. Declutter — Less Is Always More Space

This one is not a design trick, but it is the most impactful thing you can do. A small room with fewer, well-chosen objects feels bigger than the same room packed with things.

Visual noise — too many objects on shelves, too many cushions, too many plants, too many surfaces with things on them — makes a small room feel chaotic and cramped.

The goal is not minimalism for its own sake. The goal is that every object in the room has a clear reason to be there and that surfaces have a defined "done state" — a maximum number of objects that belong there.

## 10. Choose Light, Solid Upholstery

Dark upholstered furniture — a dark sofa, dark armchairs — makes a small room feel heavier. Light upholstery (cream, beige, light grey) reflects more light and takes less visual weight.

This does not mean you cannot have any colour. A terracotta cushion or a navy throw against a light sofa adds colour without adding visual weight. The large pieces should be light; the colour comes from the small pieces.

## 11. Use Glass or Acrylic Furniture

A glass coffee table or acrylic side table has almost no visual weight — you can see through it to the floor below, which maintains sightlines and keeps the room feeling open.

This works especially well for coffee tables in small living rooms where a solid wood or upholstered table would feel heavy.

**Options:** acrylic side tables from ~$60–$120 CAD on Amazon.ca. Glass coffee tables from ~$150–$350 CAD at IKEA or Wayfair Canada.

## 12. Maximize Natural Light

Natural light makes any room feel larger. Remove anything that blocks it:
- Heavy curtains during the day — swap for sheers or leave curtains open
- Furniture placed in front of windows — move it away
- Blinds that cut the window down — replace with full-height roller blinds or sheers

Also reflect it: place a mirror beside or opposite the window to bounce natural light into darker parts of the room.

![Small room with maximized natural light through sheer curtains and a strategically placed mirror](/images/blog-14-img3.jpg)

## 13. Layer Lighting for Depth

Overhead-only lighting flattens a room — it illuminates everything evenly with no shadow or depth. Layered lighting creates the illusion of dimension.

**The layered approach:**
- Ambient: floor lamp pointing upward, fills corners
- Task: table lamp or desk lamp at eye level
- Accent: LED strips behind TV or under shelves

This combination creates zones of light and shadow that make the room feel larger and more interesting. All plug-in, no installation required.

Swap all bulbs to 2700K warm white (Canadian Tire, ~$15–$25 CAD for a multipack) if you have not already.

## The Priority Order to Make a Room Look Bigger

1. **Clear the sightlines** — remove anything tall or bulky from the primary view
2. **Get the rug the right size** — front legs of furniture on the rug
3. **Add a large mirror** — opposite or beside the window
4. **Hang curtains from ceiling to floor** — immediately adds visual height
5. **Choose light upholstery** — light sofa, colour in cushions and throws

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the fastest way to make a small room look bigger?**
A large mirror opposite the main window and floor-to-ceiling curtains. Together these two changes take about an afternoon, cost under $150 CAD total, and visually expand any small room. The mirror adds perceived depth; the curtains add perceived height.

**Does dark paint make a small room look smaller?**
Generally yes. Dark walls absorb light and push visual boundaries inward. In a small room this makes it feel more confined. Light walls (white, cream, pale grey, pale sage) reflect light and make the space feel open. Renters in Canadian apartments usually do not have the option to paint, so light-coloured rugs and upholstery become more important.

**What size rug should I use in a small room?**
Larger than you think. In a living room, 160 × 230 cm is usually the minimum. The front legs of all seating furniture should be on the rug. A too-small rug makes the room feel more cramped, not less.

**Does glass furniture make a small room look bigger?**
Yes. Glass and acrylic furniture maintains sightlines — you can see through the piece rather than having it block the view. A glass coffee table takes up the same floor space as a solid one but has almost no visual weight, making the room feel more open.
